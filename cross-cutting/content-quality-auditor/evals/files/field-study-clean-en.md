# Indoor Humidity in Athens Apartments: 12 Months of Sensor Data from 38 Homes

Canonical URL: https://www.aeraslab.example/reports/athens-apartment-humidity-2026
By Elena Vasiliou, Dipl. Building Physics Engineer (NTUA) · Published 12 May 2026 · Reviewed by Dr. Kostas Marinos, mechanical engineer

**Key takeaways**

- Median winter indoor relative humidity across the 38 monitored apartments was 61%.
- Ground-floor apartments in pre-1980 buildings ran wettest: median 71% in winter.
- 22 of 38 apartments spent three or more winter months above the 60% condensation-risk threshold we adopt from the EN 16798-1 comfort-category summary cited below.
- Drying laundry indoors added a measured 8–12 percentage points of relative humidity for 4–6 hours per session.

## What this report answers

Between 1 January and 31 December 2025 we logged temperature and relative humidity every 10 minutes in 38 occupied apartments across Athens. This report answers three questions: how humid Athens apartments actually are through the year, which building types run wettest, and which occupant habits move the numbers most. It does not cover mold remediation methods, HVAC sizing, or detached houses — the sample is apartments only.

Who this is for: apartment residents deciding whether they need a dehumidifier, and building managers prioritizing envelope repairs.

## Method

- Sample: 38 occupied apartments (14 ground-floor, 24 upper-floor) in 21 buildings, recruited through two building-management firms; apartments already running a dehumidifier were excluded
- Instruments: one calibrated sensor (±2% RH stated accuracy) per main living area, logging every 10 minutes
- Period: 1 January – 31 December 2025
- Analysis: monthly medians per apartment; winter = December–February, summer = June–August

## Results

| Group | Winter median RH | Summer median RH |
|---|---|---|
| All 38 apartments | 61% | 47% |
| Ground floor, pre-1980 (n=9) | 71% | 55% |
| Ground floor, post-1980 (n=5) | 66% | 51% |
| Upper floors (n=24) | 58% | 45% |

Nine of the 38 apartments had visible mold on at least one wall at the January 2026 closing inspection; eight of those nine were in the group that spent three or more winter months above 60%.

The World Health Organization's guidance on dampness and mould links persistent indoor dampness to respiratory effects (https://who-guidelines.example.org/dampness-mould). The 60% threshold we use follows the condensation-risk range in the EN 16798-1 comfort-category summary (https://standards-summaries.example.org/en16798-comfort).

## What moved the numbers

Occupant habits mattered as much as building age. Drying laundry indoors raised relative humidity by a measured 8–12 percentage points for 4–6 hours; cooking without the extractor fan added 5–7 points for about 2 hours. Apartments that aired out for 10 minutes twice daily in winter ran a median 6 points drier than the rest of the sample.

## Limitations

One sensor per apartment cannot capture room-to-room differences; bedrooms may run wetter than our living-room readings. We did not measure CO2 or particulates. The sample skews toward central Athens, so coastal suburbs may differ. Habit data came from resident diaries, which are self-reported.

## FAQ

**Is 61% indoor humidity dangerous?** Not by itself: sustained months above 60% raise condensation risk on cold surfaces per the EN summary we cite; short peaks are normal household life.

**Do I need a dehumidifier in an upper-floor flat?** Our upper-floor winter median of 58% suggests most upper-floor apartments manage with airing habits alone; measure for two weeks before buying anything.

**What indoor humidity should I aim for?** The 40–60% band in the EN summary we cite covers comfort and condensation risk for most homes.

## Bottom line

Athens apartments in our sample run wetter in winter than the comfort band: the all-apartment median of 61% sits just above the 60% risk threshold, and ground-floor pre-1980 homes sit at a median 71%. If you live in one, two weeks of measurement will tell you whether airing habits are enough; in our data they often were not. Next step: measure first, then decide on equipment.
