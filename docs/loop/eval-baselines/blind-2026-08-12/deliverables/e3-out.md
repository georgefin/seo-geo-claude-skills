**Straight answer: nobody can commit to that date. Not me, not an agency, not Google. If someone gives you
31 October, they are giving you a number they invented, and you will own the miss on 1 November.**

Three reasons, in the order they matter:

**a) Google publishes no timeframe and states that display is not something it influences on request.**
What Google documents is that knowledge panels are "created automatically by Google Search Algorithm when
there is enough information available on the open web"; it describes the sources no more precisely than

(support.google.com/knowledgepanel/answer/9787176). There is no submission form, and no route that creates
a panel on request.

panel that belongs to a different entity sharing its name. Your own note says it: *"There is no panel
anywhere for our company to claim."*

to hand back to the board — politely, and with a substitute.

### On the consultant's advice — "just pay someone to write us a Wikipedia article"

**Do not do this.** It is wrong on three separate counts, and one of them is a policy breach:

1. **Wikipedia's conflict-of-interest policy prohibits organisations from creating or editing articles about
   themselves, and paying someone to write an article without disclosure violates policy.** The legitimate
   route, where an article is warranted, is independent coverage first and then the Requested Articles
   process — an independent editor, not a purchased one.
2. **Notability is the gate, and I cannot tell you whether we clear it.** Wikipedia's general notability
   guideline needs significant coverage in reliable sources that are independent of us, non-trivial, and not
   press releases or our own content. Neither of the two files in front of me contains a coverage inventory,
   so this is unassessed — and it is the check to run *before* anyone is paid anything, not after.
   The article is the last step of that process, never the first.
3. **A Wikipedia article is not a panel switch.** Google names neither Wikipedia nor Wikidata among its panel
   sources and publishes no measurement linking either to a panel. So the advice was: spend money, breach a
   platform policy, on a mechanism nobody has documented, to hit a date nobody can commit to.

**The nearby thing that is legitimate** is Wikidata, which is a different project with its own, looser
notability policy — read the current text at `wikidata.org/wiki/Wikidata:Notability` before starting, since
it is a community policy that changes, and do not predict the outcome internally: whether a given item
survives is the editors' call, and a board told we "should qualify" will treat a deletion as our error.

So «Φάρος» is not a branded query for this company. It is a query about a monument and an ordinary Greek

### The strategy that follows

This is the textbook collision case: a name shared with a much larger, older meaning. The response is not to
attack the collision, it is to stop entering it.

1. **Qualify the name, always, everywhere we control it.** The registry has already decided the hard part —
   ΓΕΜΗ διακριτικός τίτλος **`PHAROS MARINE DATA`**, επωνυμία **`ΦΑΡΟΣ ΝΑΥΤΙΛΙΑΚΗ ΠΛΗΡΟΦΟΡΙΚΗ ΙΔΙΩΤΙΚΗ
   ΚΕΦΑΛΑΙΟΥΧΙΚΗ ΕΤΑΙΡΕΙΑ`. Bare «Φάρος» is a short form for use *after* the full name has appeared in the
   same text, never a first mention, never a headline, never a `name` field. Note that the homepage JSON-LD
   currently publishes the bare string `Pharos` as the company's name, and the site header logo is the bare
   word «Φάρος» with the Latin name appearing nowhere in the header.
2. **Pair the name with the topic in every field we control** — the schema `description`, the profile bios,
   the page titles. «φάρος ναυτιλιακό λογισμικό» at position 3 is evidence that the name-plus-topic pairing
   already resolves; the bare name does not.
3. **Never buy, target or report on «Φάρος» or `Faros` as branded queries.** Any reporting that includes them
   will show us losing forever to a monument, a freight forwarder and a beach.
4. **Fix the identity contradictions**, because they are what makes the qualified name fragile too — the two
   JSON-LD blocks disagree on name, address, phone and founding year, and the EN page contradicts the
   registry on city and year.

*One thing your sheet flags that the board may assume exists:* there is **no trademark registration for
either the Greek or the Latin name** — nothing filed with the ΟΒΙ or the EUIPO. That is a legal-side decision
outside this memo, and I am not going to claim any search effect for it either way; I note it only so nobody
in the room assumes we already hold the name.
