    <h1>Cast Iron Skillet Seasoning: The Complete Guide</h1>
    <p>Cast iron skillet seasoning is the process of baking thin layers of oil onto the pan surface until they polymerise into a hard, slick, protective coating. Done properly, seasoning makes the skillet naturally non-stick, stops rust in its tracks, and gets better with every use.</p>

    <h2>Why Seasoning Matters</h2>
    <p>Bare cast iron starts to rust within hours of staying wet. The polymerised oil layer acts as a barrier between the metal and moisture, and it is also what lets a fried egg slide out of the pan without sticking. A skillet that is seasoned well needs no synthetic coating at all.</p>
    <p>New to cast iron? <a href="/blog/choosing-cast-iron">Click here</a> to read our buying guide before you season anything.</p>

    <h4>Choosing the Right Oil</h4>
    <p>Any neutral oil with a reasonably high smoke point will work. Grapeseed and refined sunflower oil are dependable choices. Flaxseed oil produces a very hard finish but is prone to flaking when applied too thickly, so use it sparingly if at all.</p>

    <h2>Step-by-Step Seasoning Method</h2>
    <ol>
      <li>Wash the skillet with warm water and a small amount of soap, then dry it completely on the hob.</li>
      <li>Wipe a thin film of oil over every surface, inside and out, then buff it back until the pan looks almost dry.</li>
      <li>Bake the skillet upside down at high heat for an hour, then let it cool in the oven.</li>
      <li>Repeat the oiling and baking two or three more times for a deeper base layer.</li>
    </ol>
    <img src="/uploads/IMG_2847.jpg" width="800" height="533">
    <p>The thin-film rule is the one people break most often. If the oil pools anywhere, the finish will cure sticky instead of hard.</p>
    <img src="/uploads/skillet-oven.jpg" alt="" width="800" height="533">

    <h2>Common Seasoning Mistakes</h2>
    <p>Thick oil layers, skipping the buffing step, and pulling the pan out of the oven too early are the three mistakes we see most. All three leave a tacky surface that grabs food instead of releasing it. If your seasoning has already gone patchy, scrub the loose flakes away and re-season from step one rather than oiling over the damage.</p>
    <img src="/uploads/flaxseed-oil-flaking.jpg" alt="Flaking patches of seasoning on a cast iron skillet caused by overly thick flaxseed oil layers" width="800" height="533">
    <p>Once your base layer is down, day-to-day care matters more than any single seasoning session. See our guide to <a href="/blog/cast-iron-cleaning">cleaning cast iron without stripping the seasoning</a> for the routine we recommend.</p>
